01 · Research · Lead story

Teaching machines to read how humans move

With Dr Carlo Campagnoli and Dr Elham Mirfarah, University of Leeds · Paper in preparation

Does the way we reach for an object carry traces of what we can see? My MSc dissertation put that question to 2,600+ trials from 23 participants, decoding visual feedback from grip-aperture kinematics.

The honest answer: a modest but real signal. A well-validated logistic regression reached 64.2% accuracy, and deep sequence models did not reliably beat it.

I am now extending the work into a research paper, Hidden Grip Strategies, unifying reach, grasp and transport data in one pipeline, and scoping a follow-up on visuomotor control after stroke.

01Did rapid growth change Netflix?Analysis2026
Overview

Between 2016 and 2019 Netflix added five times more titles a year. I asked whether that growth changed what it added: more TV shows, more international productions, newer releases. I tested each idea on 5,884 titles with chi-square, Kruskal–Wallis and logistic regression.

The challenge

The data is a January 2020 snapshot, so titles removed before then are missing. Every finding had to be framed around what the snapshot can and cannot show.

What I learned

Let the data disagree. Two of my three predictions were not supported: TV's share showed no steady trend, additions outside the US peaked at 61% in 2018 before falling to 52%, and additions actually got older.

02What makes a movie popular?Film2026
Overview

Is a film's popularity explained by its genre, how recent it is, or how well it is rated? I cleaned and visualised 9,800 popular films from TMDB.

The challenge

When I audited my own notebook I found four issues, including a binning step that silently dropped the 100 lowest-rated films.

What I learned

Checking my own work is part of the analysis. The corrected results show recency matters most (median popularity rises from 22 for 2010s films to 35 for the 2020s), rating barely matters (ρ = 0.13), and all four factors together explain only 30% of popularity.

03Growth vs emissionsClimate2026
Overview

Which countries grew their economies while cutting CO₂ between 2005 and 2019? Using Our World in Data figures for 117 countries, I classified each on both territorial and consumption-based emissions.

The challenge

Counting the emissions in imported goods changes the answer. Switzerland's territorial emissions fell 20%, but its consumption emissions rose 12%, and consumption figures are modelled estimates.

What I learned

26 countries, including the UK, Germany and the US, decoupled on both measures. Counting imports shrank the list from 34 to 28, and decoupling was overwhelmingly a rich-country result: 62% of the richest third, none of the poorest.

04The smoking penalty above BMI 30Health2026
Overview

How much more are smokers charged for medical care, and is the penalty the same for everyone? I fitted regression models on 1,337 adults, adjusting for age, sex, children and region, and checked them with 2,000 bootstrap resamples.

The challenge

The penalty was not the same for everyone, so a single smoking effect hid the real pattern. The dataset is also simulated from census figures, so results are patterns in the data, not real-world causes.

What I learned

One well-chosen interaction term mattered more than extra complexity. The penalty more than doubled above BMI 30, from about $13,400 to $33,200 a year, and explained variation rose from 75% to 86%.

05Who is about to leave?Business2026
Overview

Which customers are about to leave, and can a simple model find them in time? Using IBM's Telco data of 7,043 customers, I compared logistic regression with gradient boosting under cross-validation and a held-out test set.

The challenge

The model had to be one a business could explain and act on, not just the most accurate. It is a sample dataset, so results show association rather than cause.

What I learned

Simple can be enough. The explainable model matched gradient boosting (ROC-AUC 0.85 vs 0.84) and was well calibrated. Contacting the riskiest 20% of customers would reach half of those who leave, 2.5 times better than random.

01ResultWise — no follow-up left behindAI in Healthcare Hackathon · Team of fourOct 2026
Overview

Built at the AI in Healthcare Hackathon, Leeds, on 24–25 October 2026. AI reads breast cancer scan reports, plain rules track bookings at both hospitals, and a clinician approves every message before it reaches the patient in her own language. I set up the repository, built the first working prototype, the clinician review flow and the evaluation, and maintained the web app the team demoed.

The challenge

Scan follow-ups can slip through when care moves between hospitals, because each system only sees its own bookings. Anything reaching a patient had to stay under a clinician's control.

What I learned

On 10 held-out synthetic reports the AI found every follow-up while keyword rules found 22%, but rules were more precise (100% vs 90%). That is why the clinician review step matters. It is a prototype on synthetic data, not clinically validated.
